# Resampled-comparison report

Loaded 838 scenarios from `scan-cache.json`.

This report compares each (lens, focal, magnification) capture against
sibling "resized" trees generated by `resize_bulk.py` (in this folder).
The resize step is the experimental analogue of "if I'd shot this same
subject at a different magnification, what would the same crop look
like?" — and lets us ask whether stronger glass adds detail beyond what
upsampling a weaker capture can fake.

The tables below are auto-generated by `summarise.py`. Section 0 captures
the qualitative findings and rules-of-thumb that came out of running and
discussing the comparison; they belong with the data, not in slack
history.

## 0. Findings & rules-of-thumb

### 0.0 Crop dimensions: 512 x 512 (was 500 x 500)

As of 2026-05 the crop size used by both the live runner
(`run-lens-test/sharpness/crops.py`) and the batch scripts
(`make-9-crops.sh`, `make-17-crops.sh`, `resize_bulk.py`) is **512 x 512**.
The 12-pixel-per-side bump from the legacy 500 is what enables
**lossless JPEG cropping** end-to-end: 512 is a multiple of 16, the MCU
size for 4:2:0-subsampled sources, so `TurboJPEG.crop_multiple` can
re-pack encoded MCU blocks into the output JPEGs without decoding back
to RGB and re-encoding. The crop output inherits the source's encoded
quality exactly — no generation loss.

Consequences for the file-size sharpness proxy:

- Cropped files are ~4.9 % larger by pixel count, but **smaller** in
  bytes for the same scene because there's no second-generation JPEG
  compression. Net file size shifts depend on scene content; expect
  individual crops in the 50-100 KB range vs the previous 100-150 KB
  band.
- Within-dataset comparisons stay clean (every crop is generated
  through the same pipeline at the same dims).
- Cross-version comparisons against pre-2026-05 caches don't transfer
  numerically (we already invalidated the cache once for Catrom; this
  is the second invalidation in the same week).

Shared origin math lives in `lens_test_detect_core.mcu`:
`compute_crop_origins` for the 9 full-frame positions and
`compute_dx_origins` for the 8 DX inner positions. Both snap each
returned origin to a 16-multiple via `align_down`, so any future
sensor resolution stays MCU-aligned automatically.

### 0.1 Resampler choice: Catrom over Lanczos

`resize-from-to.sh` and `resize_bulk.py` both upsample with **Catmull-Rom
bicubic** (Pillow's `BICUBIC`, equivalent kernel to GraphicsMagick's
`Catrom`). The original script used Lanczos. The empirical reason for
switching:

- Same combo, same target, before vs after:
  - 24-120 + MCON-35, 1_1-resized F8 (Lanczos): **113,917 bytes**
  - 24-120 + MCON-35, 1_1-resized F8 (Catrom): **46,021 bytes**
- That's a ~60 % drop in file size from changing only the resample
  kernel.

The drop is Lanczos's Gibbs ringing around edges. The ring halos compress
poorly under JPEG and inflate file sizes by amounts unrelated to real
captured detail. Catrom has small local overshoot but no long-range
ringing, so the JPEG-size proxy reflects the actual optical signal more
faithfully. Relative rankings between combos are preserved across the
two kernels but with Catrom the gaps are wider — the comparison is more
diagnostic.

See [`../SHARPNESS_METRICS.md`](../SHARPNESS_METRICS.md) for the broader
context on why file size is a problematic proxy across different framings
in the first place, and what better metrics would be.

### 0.2 Stronger optical magnification beats upsampling — monotonically

Within every lens family, sorting combos by native magnification (high
→ low) almost perfectly matches sorting by 2:1-resized centre file size
(high → low). The headline cases:

- **24-120 + Raynox M-250 (+8D, native 1:1.12)** beats the bare 24-120
  upsampled 4.94× by **74 %** in 2:1-resized F8 file size.
- **200/4 alone (native 1:1)** + 6T (+2.9D, native 1.7:1) — the 6T capture
  upsampled 1.18× to 2:1 beats the bare 200/4 upsampled 2.0× by **35 %**
  in centre file size at F11.
- The 24-120 family has 17 measured points spanning M-250 (+8D) to bare
  lens, with a smoothly monotone curve except for one outlier (10 mm
  extension at 120 mm focal — see §0.6).

Stronger glass adds real captured detail that no upsampler can recover.
Diminishing returns set in: going from +1.5D to +2.9D buys ~3 %; going
from +2.9D to +4.4D buys another ~5 %; going from +4.4D to +8D buys ~8 %.

### 0.3 The "strong + long" diopter rule, and its corollaries

**Rule:** image quality with a close-up filter degrades as
`D × f_camera` grows. Doubling either diopter strength or host focal
length roughly doubles the optical penalty.

Corollaries from the data:

- **6T (+2.9D) is catastrophic on the Z 100-400** — at 2:1-resized F11
  it scores **17,332** vs 53,175 for the 5T (+1.5D) on the same lens.
  The bare 100-400 upsampled 5.2× (**24,067**) literally outperforms the
  6T upsampled 2.4×.
- **6T is the *best* combination on the AF Micro 200/4** — at the same
  test framing it scores **67,269**, top of its leaderboard. Same
  diopter, ~half the host focal length, completely different result.
- **The Olympus MCON-35 (+2.4D) lands between weak and strong diopters
  on the 100-400.** Modern multi-element design with off-axis correction
  for a wider FOV than the 6T, which gives it more headroom when used
  outside its target focal range — see §0.8 for its history.

The rule is much weaker for **achromatic doublets** than for single-
element diopters. All the diopters in this dataset (3T/4T/5T/6T, Canon
500D, MCON-35, Raynox M-150/M-250) are achromats. Stacking two singletons
on a long lens is genuinely bad; stacking two matched achromats on the
focal range they were designed for can be excellent (§0.4).

### 0.4 Stacking achromats — when it works

Conventional wisdom is "don't stack diopters". That comes from the era
of single-element close-up filters where each stacked filter compounds
chromatic aberration through 4 air-glass surfaces.

On the 200/4 at native 2:1, the **5T+6T and 6T+5T stacks** score **142,530
and 144,783** at F11 — within ~5 % of the single 6T's score and at
**higher magnification**. That's a positive result.

Why it works here: 5T and 6T are Nikon's matched-system achromat pair
designed for the 70–200 mm range on the same 62 mm thread, with matched
coatings. When stacked they behave more like one ~3.5-element close-up
optic than two independent filters. The mounting-order effect (§6 of
`../ANALYSIS_NOTES.md`) shows the stack is sensitive to which element
is closer to the lens, but the absolute IQ cost of stacking is small on
their target focal range.

So the refined rule:

- Stack matched achromats designed for the same system, within their
  intended focal range — OK.
- Stack arbitrary single-element diopters — bad.
- Stack achromats *outside* their intended focal range (5T+6T on a 400
  mm host) — would inherit the same per-element penalty as the 6T alone.

### 0.5 Extension tubes vs diopters — the FE/CRC distinction

Extension tubes and diopters interact differently with floating-element
/ close-range-correction designs:

- **Extension tube**: pushes the image plane back. The lens's focusing
  helicoid is unchanged but the actual image distance is longer than the
  helicoid encoder thinks. FE/CRC groups are positioned for the encoder
  reading → wrong position for the actual geometry → residual spherical
  aberration and field curvature. **FE-mismatch is a real concern for
  tubes.**
- **Diopter**: lens-to-sensor distance unchanged. The diopter only
  modifies the *object plane* the lens sees. The lens's focusing
  helicoid is at the position the diopter's effective object distance
  calls for, and the FE/CRC group is correctly positioned for that
  helicoid reading. **FE is not perturbed by diopters.**

This explains a result that should otherwise be confusing: the 200/4 D
(which has CRC) performs beautifully with the 6T. If FE mattered for
diopters, that combination would be bad. It isn't.

So the working rules are:

- For **diopters**: `D × f_camera`, diopter design envelope, achromat vs
  singleton. FE/CRC of the host lens is largely irrelevant.
- For **extension tubes**: FE/CRC of the host *matters*. Non-FE designs
  (24-120, 70-180 in this dataset) tube well. FE/CRC primes tube less
  well outside the focus range their FE is calibrated for. Dedicated
  macros with FE designed for the close-focus regime (MC 105) tolerate
  tubes within that regime, because the tube pushes the lens *further
  into* its calibrated zone, not out of it.

Lens-by-lens FE/CRC inventory in this dataset:

| Lens | FE/CRC? | Tubes? |
|---|---|---|
| Nikkor 55/2.8 AIS | Yes (CRC) | Within macro range, OK |
| AF Micro 200/4 D IF-ED | Yes (CRC) | Within macro range, OK |
| Sigma 150/2.8 HSM (non-OS) | Yes (FE) | Within macro range, OK |
| Sigma 150/2.8 OS | Yes (FE) | Within macro range, OK |
| AF Zoom-Micro 70-180 | No | Very forgiving |
| Z 24-120 f/4 S | No (IF only; not FE) | Forgiving |
| Z MC 105/2.8 S | Yes (FE for macro) | Forgiving in macro range |
| Z 100-400 | Yes (multiple) | Not in this dataset, but expected stiff |

Note: IF (internal focus) ≠ FE. The 24-120 has IF but no floating
elements; it tolerates tubes well precisely because there's no FE group
to mis-position.

### 0.6 The 24-120 + 10 mm extension at 120 mm focal — outlier

This combination is anomalously low across all apertures (e.g. F8 native
**81,053**, vs ~130 k expected from its neighbours). The previous
explanation ("FE-mismatch") was wrong because the 24-120 has no floating
elements. More likely candidates:

- 10 mm of extension on 120 mm gives only ×0.083 added magnification.
  The lens may not have been able to focus close enough to put the wafer
  pattern in frame, so the centre crop hit off-target area.
- Possible focus / framing miscalibration in that specific session.

The 26 mm extension at 120 mm shows none of this dip (F8 = 131,692), so
it's a problem specific to the 10 mm at long focal lengths.

### 0.7 26 mm extension on 24-120 competing with diopters

In the 24-120 family, the **26 mm extension at 50 / 70 / 110 mm focal
lengths** lands in positions 2–4 of the 2:1-resized leaderboard, beating
most diopter combinations at this lens. Extension tubes contribute *no
chromatic aberration* (no glass), so for a non-FE host they're a clean
magnification boost.

Why this isn't the universal answer:

- Costs light (proportional to `(1 + tube/f)^2`).
- Doesn't help on FE/CRC hosts outside their calibration range (§0.5).
- The 26 mm tube on the Z mount needs Z-mount-aware tubes (the Kenko Z
  10/16 mm pair, Fotodiox 35 mm, etc.) since Nikon does not make Z
  extension tubes themselves — see §0.9.

### 0.8 Olympus MCON-35 — era and design context

The MCON-35 is from the bridge/ZLR era — late 1990s / early 2000s. It
was designed for the Olympus C-series Camedia high-end compacts
(C-2500L, C-3030/3040 etc.) and the E-10 / E-20 ZLRs, all of which used
a 62 mm front filter thread on a zoom that went wide (~35 mm equivalent
at the short end). It is **not** a 4/3rds lens, and **not** a film-era
product.

The stepped **62 mm rear / 72 mm front** geometry is a giveaway for that
context: the host lens's wide-FOV light cone needs room to exit the
diopter without clipping the rim. By keeping the front element 10 mm
larger than the mount thread, Olympus gave the off-axis rays clearance
to leave the diopter cleanly — the same trick Canon used on the 500D-77
and Raynox uses on the MSN-202.

Implications for this dataset:

- The wider front aperture doesn't add **light** when the lens is mounted
  on a 100-400 (limiting aperture is at the 62 mm rear thread). But the
  diopter's optical prescription was computed for a wider FOV than the
  6T's was, which gives it more headroom when used outside its target
  focal range. This is why it lands between weak achromats and the 6T
  on the 100-400, despite being only marginally weaker than the 6T as
  measured in diopters.
- On the 100-400 at 400 mm f/5.6 the entrance pupil is ~71 mm. Any 62 mm
  diopter (MCON-35, 6T, etc.) will **physically vignette**. At f/8 the
  pupil drops below 62 mm and vignetting clears. The "use diopters
  stopped down" guidance has a hard geometric basis on long-tele hosts.

The 6T predates the MCON-35 by about a decade — it was introduced with
the AF Micro 200/4 D in 1990.

### 0.9 Nikon does not make Z-mount extension tubes

Earlier drafts of this analysis claimed Nikon produces a Z-mount tube
set; that is incorrect. As of 2026 the Z extension tube market is
entirely third-party — Fotodiox, JJC, Kenko, Meike, Viltrox, Vello, etc.
Nikon themselves have stayed out, just as they did for the F-mount AF
era (the manual PK series from the F-mount film era is the last tube
set they themselves shipped). The reason is most plausibly margin
cannibalisation: a $50 tube competes with a $700-1000 MC 105 / MC 50
macro, and Nikon would rather steer buyers to the latter.

Source check: [Thom Hogan's Z-mount tube
list](https://www.zsystemuser.com/z-mount-lenses/z-mount-adapters/other-adapters/extension-tubes.html)
agrees — no Nikon-branded entry.

The user's working setup for this dataset is Kenko 10 mm + 16 mm pair
plus a Fotodiox 35 mm.
